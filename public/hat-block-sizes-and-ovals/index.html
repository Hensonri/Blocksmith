Fit before filament

Hat block size and head oval are not the same thing.

Circumference tells you how much distance exists around the opening. Head oval tells you how that distance is distributed front-to-back and side-to-side. A good printable hat block needs both.

Start with the finished size

Hat sizing systems can be confusing because the same head circumference may be expressed as a United States hat size, a metric circumference, or an inch measurement. Blocksmith Works keeps millimetres as the internal geometry source of truth and presents familiar size labels to the maker. That avoids a common CAD problem: converting units repeatedly until rounding errors begin to stack up.

The important measurement is the intended finished circumference at the sweatband area. That is the fit target. It is not automatically the exact outside circumference of every blocking tool because material thickness, sweatband allowance, and the particular blocking workflow can introduce separate clearances. Blocksmith keeps those ideas separate rather than burying them in a mysterious scale factor.

Why circumference alone is not enough

Imagine two loops of string with exactly the same length. One can be nearly round while the other is stretched longer from front to back and narrower from side to side. The perimeter can remain the same even though the shape is very different. Human heads behave the same way.

That is why a hat can be technically the correct size and still create pressure at the forehead and back, or feel loose at the sides. The circumference is right, but the distribution is wrong.

Regular OvalThe head is longer front-to-back than side-to-side, but not extremely so.
Long OvalThe front-to-back dimension is more pronounced for the same general circumference.

A traditional hat oval is not necessarily a perfect ellipse

This is an important design detail for 3D printed hat blocks. A mathematical ellipse is easy to generate, but easy does not automatically mean anatomically correct. Traditional hat-block practice often produces an opening that is fuller through the diagonal regions than a simple ellipse. In other words, it can look slightly squarer or more generous at the four diagonal quadrants while still remaining smooth.

A simple ellipse can match overall length, width, and circumference and still put too much curvature in the wrong places. Blocksmith therefore treats the current Round, Regular Oval, and Long Oval footprints as geometry that must be validated against measured traditional forms and real head tracings rather than assuming a single equation is universally correct.

Blocksmith validation direction: compare generated Regular Oval and Long Oval footprints with traditional multi-arc hat ovals and with measured historical blocks. Size labels are useful; measured geometry is the deciding evidence.

What does Regular Oval mean?

There is no single worldwide CAD definition of Regular Oval shared by every hat-block maker. Commercial makers have historically used their own patterns, templates, and naming conventions. A useful fitting reference is that Regular Oval is commonly noticeably longer front-to-back than side-to-side, while Long Oval increases that difference further. Those ranges should be treated as fitting guidance, not as a universal mathematical rule.

For a maker, the practical question is simpler: where does a well-fitting hat contact the head? If an existing hat fits evenly without obvious side gaps or front-and-back pressure, its opening can provide useful evidence about the wearer's head oval. A conformer, tracing, or carefully captured head shape provides even better information when precision matters.

How oval affects the block above the opening

The base footprint should not suddenly turn into a different shape as the crown rises. A printable block needs a smooth transition from the selected head oval into the crown sidewalls and upper curves. Taper, crown height, shoulder radius, and top profile all interact with that footprint.

This is one reason Blocksmith does not simply scale an entire model in X or Y to create another oval. Non-uniform scaling can change upper radii, wall slopes, top proportions, socket geometry, and other features that were not supposed to change. The better approach is to generate the intended footprint first and build the governed crown surface from it.

Hat size versus sweatband allowance

A leather sweatband occupies space. Some hat-making workflows intentionally block a crown slightly larger so the finished sweatband brings the internal fit back toward the target size. That allowance is a separate decision from head oval and from brim-opening clearance.

Blocksmith's longer-term geometry direction is to make sweatband allowance user-selectable, with a Standard choice as the default, and record the chosen allowance in the underside identification. Keeping the allowance explicit makes it easier to reproduce a successful block and prevents hidden sizing assumptions.

Hat size versus brim-block opening

A brim flange or brim block has another clearance problem: the felt itself has thickness where the crown passes through the opening. A crown block sized to the wearer and a flange opening used for blocking-in-one may therefore need different dimensions even though both relate to the same finished hat.

That is why Blocksmith keeps crown sizing, sweatband allowance, and brim-opening material clearance as separate variables. Combining them into a single unexplained “make it bigger” setting would make the result harder to understand and harder to validate.

How to choose your Blocksmith starting shape

  1. Use the wearer's known finished hat size or a careful head circumference measurement.
  2. If a well-fitting hat already exists, observe whether its opening is close to round, regular oval, or distinctly long oval.
  3. Choose the closest Blocksmith oval as the starting point rather than forcing circumference to solve the shape problem.
  4. Generate the model and inspect both the front-to-back and side-to-side dimensions.
  5. For a critical custom fit, validate against a tracing, conformer result, scan, or known-good hat opening before committing expensive material.

Do not resize a hat block by blindly scaling the STL

Scaling a downloaded STL seems convenient, but it changes everything in the model at once. A 5% scale increase changes the circumference, crown height, radii, stand features, lettering, socket diameters, and wall dimensions. Non-uniform scaling can distort them differently in each direction.

A parametric generator is useful precisely because it can change the variable you intended to change while preserving the variables that should remain governed. That principle is central to both Blocksmith crown blocks and the historical brim-block work now under development.

Generate from the fit outward

The best starting point is the real wearer, not an arbitrary STL size. Once the size and oval are correct, crown height and style become design choices rather than attempts to compensate for a poor fit.
