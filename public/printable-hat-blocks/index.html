A free 3D printable hat block generator

Traditional hat blocks are specialized tools. They also tend to be expensive, difficult to find in an exact size and oval, and costly to collect when a maker needs several shapes. Blocksmith Works approaches that problem from the other direction: generate the tooling you need and print it on a machine you already have access to.

The Crown Workshop is designed around millinery measurements instead of generic CAD controls. A maker starts with a finished hat size or circumference, chooses the head shape, sets the open-crown height and profile, and generates a full-scale model. The goal is not to replace the craft of blocking, steaming, creasing, trimming, sewing, or finishing. The goal is to make the physical tooling more accessible.

What can you change?

A useful hat block is more than a circumference wrapped around a cylinder. The way the opening carries through the sidewall, shoulder, and top influences how the felt sits and how much shaping room remains for the finished crease. Blocksmith keeps the common choices understandable while protecting the geometry underneath.

The workshop supports user-selected crown height and multiple crown-top profiles. Current governed profiles include Full Dome, Rounded Open, Classic Open, and Shallow Dome. The system also keeps the upper transition smooth rather than joining a straight wall to the crown with a sharp corner. That matters because the working surface is the part the felt actually touches.

Hat size and head shape are treated as separate ideas. Two heads can have the same circumference and still need different front-to-back and side-to-side proportions. That is why Blocksmith does not treat “size 7 3/8” as enough information by itself. Read the hat block sizes and head ovals guide for a plain-language explanation.

Designed to become a physical workshop tool

The STL is only one step. Blocksmith also includes a printable adjustable block stand so the generated tooling can be used at a practical working height. The crown system uses a governed five-socket interface, and the current deep-socket geometry provides 2.75-inch socket depth for compatibility and grip. The printed stand stud is 1.50 inches tall overall.

Every released geometry change is treated as something that has to survive more than a nice on-screen preview. Meshes are checked for printable, closed geometry; real prints are used to verify fit and surface behavior; and community feedback is used to find practical problems that CAD alone may miss.

Heat matters. A printable model is not automatically a steam-safe hat block. PLA softens at relatively low temperatures and should be treated as a prototype material around sustained steam or direct iron heat. Material choice, protective wrapping, exposure time, and technique all matter. See the materials guide before using a printed block around heat.

How to go from measurement to a printed block

  1. Choose the finished hat size or enter the circumference.
  2. Select the appropriate head oval rather than assuming every head is a simple ellipse.
  3. Set the crown height, top profile, and taper for the kind of open crown you want to shape later.
  4. Generate the print file and verify the dimensions in your slicer before printing.
  5. Choose print material and settings appropriate for the heat and load the tool will see.
  6. Inspect the finished print before putting felt on it.

The full process is covered in How to 3D Print a Custom Hat Block.

Why Blocksmith Works is free

Hat making already has a learning curve. Access to tooling should not be another barrier if a maker has the ability to print it. Blocksmith Works is built around a simple public promise: Free to use. No paywalls. Built for hatmakers. Optional community support can help with hosting, testing materials, and future development, but the core workshop is intended to remain accessible.

The project is also expanding beyond crown blocks. Brim blocks, historical flange preservation, additional crown profiles, and other printable millinery tools are being developed and tested. They are released only after the geometry and real-world use are convincing enough to call them ready.
