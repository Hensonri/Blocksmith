How to 3D print a custom hat block.

The useful part is not simply getting an STL. A dependable hat block starts with the right measurements, survives slicer checks, prints at the intended scale, and is inspected before felt ever touches it.

Generate a Hat BlockAbout the Generator

1. Start with the finished hat size

Begin with the size the finished hat is meant to fit, not with a random downloaded model that happens to be close. Blocksmith Works can start from a finished hat size or circumference. The workshop keeps the geometry in millimetres internally so the model has one consistent source of truth even if you prefer to work in inches.

Then choose the head shape. Circumference alone does not tell you whether the wearer is Round, Regular Oval, or Long Oval. A block can have the correct circumference and still distribute that circumference poorly around the head. If you are unsure why this matters, read Hat Block Sizes and Head Ovals before generating the file.

2. Choose an open-crown geometry you can shape later

Set the crown height you need for the intended finished hat. A fedora that will receive a deep crease needs enough open-crown height to give the maker room to work. Western shapes, shallow styles, and novelty forms can require very different amounts of material above the finished crease.

Blocksmith's governed crown-top profiles give you useful starting points without requiring CAD knowledge. The important idea is that the working surface stays smooth through the sidewall-to-top transition. Sharp corners, abrupt fillets, or accidental flats can telegraph into felt and make the block harder to use.

3. Generate the print file

Use the online workshop or the downloadable edition. The online version runs in a modern browser and does not require a sign-up. The desktop editions are available for Windows, macOS, and Linux. Generate the crown block only after the size, oval, height, profile, and taper look correct in the preview.

Blocksmith crown exports are intended to be full scale. That sounds obvious, but it is worth stating because scale mistakes are one of the easiest ways to waste a large print. The first thing you should verify in the slicer is the overall size.

4. Verify the model in your slicer before printing

Do not treat the slicer as a formality. Inspect the model from several angles and confirm the reported dimensions. Make sure the block sits on the intended printable base, the mesh is not being repaired into a different shape, and the five stand sockets are visible and remain open.

ScaleConfirm the model is full size and the slicer did not convert units incorrectly.
OrientationUse the intended flat print base rather than tilting the working surface unnecessarily.
SeamA random seam has produced a smoother real-world Blocksmith crown than a single aligned raised seam.
LayeringVariable layer height can improve smoothness on upper curves when your slicer supports it.

Before committing a large amount of filament, check the estimated print time and material. If anything looks surprising, stop and investigate. A ten-second check is cheaper than discovering a scale or orientation error halfway through a full-size crown.

5. Choose material for the way you will actually use the block

A material that prints beautifully is not automatically suitable for repeated steam or iron exposure. PLA is easy to print and useful for prototypes, fit checks, and learning, but sustained heat can soften it. PETG is tougher but still should not be treated as automatically steam-safe. Higher-temperature materials can offer more margin, but they also have printer, enclosure, drying, and nozzle requirements of their own.

Use the 3D Printing Materials for Hat Blocks guide to choose based on expected heat, printer capability, and frequency of use. Regardless of filament, avoid resting a hot iron directly on a printed block and consider a protective wrap when working with moisture, dye, or stiffener.

6. Print for a smooth working surface

The felt-contact surface matters more than cosmetic details on the underside. Use enough walls and internal structure to keep the block stiff while it is being pulled, tied, handled, and exposed to moisture. The exact wall count and infill should be treated as printer- and material-dependent rather than a universal magic number.

For large tooling, favor dependable adhesion and dimensional accuracy over aggressive speed. A surface that does not need sanding is ideal because sanding changes dimensions and can introduce unevenness. One full-size Blocksmith crown printed with a random seam and appropriate adaptive layering came off the machine smooth enough to use without sanding, which is the standard worth aiming for.

7. Inspect the print before putting felt on it

Run your hands over the working surface in strong side light. Look for layer shifts, raised seams, blobs, sharp transitions, cracks, or local distortion. Verify the block did not warp at the base and that the stand sockets are clean. Read the underside identification directly from the bottom; it should identify the block without interfering with the sockets.

If the surface has a defect you can feel with a fingertip, assume damp felt may be able to record it. Fix or reprint the tool before a valuable felt becomes the test article.

8. Use heat and steam conservatively

Hat making uses moisture, heat, tension, and time. Printed plastics respond to those same conditions. Warm the felt rather than intentionally heating the plastic, keep the iron off the block itself, and do not leave a softened print under heavy asymmetric load. If a block will see repeated steam cycles, validate the chosen material with test pieces before trusting a large tool.

PLA reminder: treat PLA as a prototype or controlled-use material around sustained steam and direct iron heat. If a print will become routine shop tooling, choose and test a material with the required heat margin.

9. Record what worked

Save the size, head oval, profile, material, slicer settings, seam strategy, and any adjustments that produced a good result. A repeatable process turns a one-off successful print into useful shop knowledge. Blocksmith is being developed around that same idea: trust the craft, but verify the geometry and the physical result.
